A crash count is not a crash rate. Federal crash reports are useful for finding incidents, but NHTSA says the counts are not normalized or adjusted by any measure of exposure, such as miles driven. [3]

04 A SECOND PAIR OF EYES

What does independent research add?

In a 2026 study, IIHS compared Waymo’s driverless crash involvement with human drivers in the same areas and years. The study found a 68% lower rate of police-reportable crash involvement per mile for the Waymo operations studied. [5]

That is evidence about those deployments and that outcome—not a direct test of Tesla FSD. As the researchers put it, These results apply only to the L4 vehicles in driverless operation that were studied. [5]

Separate IIHS research on partial automation in BMW and Nissan vehicles found little evidence of an extra crash-prevention benefit beyond crash-avoidance features. That finding is a caution about the category, not a Tesla-specific estimate. [6]

The NTSB also warns that humans can become poor monitors of automation. Rare severe crashes require more than mileage alone to evaluate, according to RAND’s safety research. [7][8]

05 TAKE THIS WITH YOU

A five-question
safety-claim check.

Use it the next time a company, article or social post calls a vehicle “safer.”

  1. 01Who was responsible for driving?Supervised assistance or rider-only operation?
  2. 02Where and when were the miles driven?Same streets, traffic, weather and period?
  3. 03Which crashes count?All contacts, police reports, airbag deployments or injuries?
  4. 04What is the comparison group?Other Teslas, local human drivers or a national average?
  5. 05How much exposure is behind the rate?Ask for miles, definitions and an independent check.

The practical conclusion: We can learn from each company’s data and independent studies, but a fair Tesla–Waymo verdict needs matched driving conditions, supervision, crash definitions and exposure. [3][5]
